Headline: The Case Against the Usual View of AI in Customer Support

The Case Against the Usual View of AI in Customer Support

By a Head of Support Operations with 12 years running support teams at consumer apps. Written for support leaders at consumer apps with growing ticket backlogs.

Support automation should be measured by tickets resolved, not tickets deflected. For support leaders at consumer apps with growing ticket backlogs, the measure to watch is automated resolution rate.

The argument

The argument of this piece rests on one point: support automation should be measured by tickets resolved, not tickets deflected. For support leaders at consumer apps with growing ticket backlogs, an idea like this is only worth the time if it shows up in the numbers the work is judged on, automated resolution rate, so that is where it is tested here. The sections below take it in order: how the parts fit, what the evidence shows, the objection it has to survive and a way to try it on your own work.

What the evidence shows

Supplied result, not independently verified: 45% of tickets resolved without a human in 60 days at one food delivery company.

Evidence of this kind is strongest when it looks like this: results on an evaluation set built from your own history, then live results with human review switched on.

What the evidence shows, and what it does not

The evidence above is a supplied result that still needs source verification. Check whether its scope and comparison support the position at all. It does not establish causation or predict what will happen in your own operation. Evidence to assess with support leaders at consumer apps with growing ticket backlogs would include results on an evaluation set built from your own history, then live results with human review switched on.

The objection: AI gets answers wrong

A reader may ask: "AI gets answers wrong". A fair way to settle it is to agree an evaluation on your own history before go-live, with a person approving any action that moves money or changes a record.

Who has to agree

Confirm who can approve, review and use the proposed change. A possible role map to check is: The owner of the workflow being automated signs; the data or AI lead evaluates the model; security and legal review data use and privacy; the operations team that hands work to the AI uses it. The actual people and approval rights need confirmation.

What to do next

Measure it on automated resolution rate, before and after the change.

Then take this question into the next meeting: "Which cases or decisions would you trust an AI to handle first, and which never?"

The takeaway

If you keep one thing from this piece, keep the point it opened with, and the test that checks it. If "AI gets answers wrong" is a real concern, agree what evidence would address it before proposing a change.

What the Objections to AI in Customer Support Miss

Part 2 of 3, by a Head of Support Operations with 12 years running support teams at consumer apps.

This picks up from "The Case Against the Usual View of AI in Customer Support" and takes the same position one step further.

The claim

This piece makes one claim, about the position set out in the first article. It stands or falls on automated resolution rate, the measures support leaders at consumer apps with growing ticket backlogs already report on. What follows sets the claim next to the rest of the position, the proof and the strongest objection, and ends with a test you can run.

The objection: data privacy

Another possible concern for support leaders at consumer apps with growing ticket backlogs is "Data privacy". A fair way to settle it is to ask the vendor to state exactly where data is processed and stored, what is used for training (if anything) and how it can be deleted.

A supplied result to verify

Return to the earlier supplied result, which still needs independent verification: 45% of tickets resolved without a human in 60 days at one food delivery company. Check the result scope and source before applying it to article 2.

Reading the evidence

Read the evidence for what it is: a supplied result that still needs source verification. Ask who supplied it, how it was measured and which alternative explanation could fit. Retain its limits instead of treating it as proof of the whole argument.

Putting it to work

To put this to work, start where the cost of the problem is easiest to see. Choose one piece of work where automated resolution rate is already tracked, write down today's figure and the period it covers, and agree who will read the result. Agree an approved test on that piece of work, with a stop rule and a reviewer. Record other changes that could affect the comparison; a before and after result alone cannot establish cause.

Where to start

Start with one team and one piece of work, and write down where it stands today.

Then take this question into the next meeting: "How would you judge that an answer is right: who reviews it today and against what?"

In short

To close, use a bounded test to decide what to investigate next. Check whether "Data privacy" is an actual concern, then agree what evidence would address it.

Headline: What to Do First About AI in Customer Support

What to Do First About AI in Customer Support

Part 3 of 3, by a Head of Support Operations with 12 years running support teams at consumer apps.

This picks up from "What the Objections to AI in Customer Support Miss" and takes the same position one step further.

Why it matters

Here the claim is narrower than in the pieces before. The reason it matters is practical. Support leaders at consumer apps with growing ticket backlogs are asked about automated resolution rate, and a change that does not move those is hard to defend in a review. The rest of this piece shows how the claim connects to the other parts, where the evidence stops and how to put it to work.

The objection: we can build this on a model API ourselves

A reader may ask: "We can build this on a model API ourselves". A fair way to settle it is to compare the full cost of building and maintaining evaluation, guardrails and integrations, not the model price.

The first thing to try

Choose one relevant case and record the decision it could inform before proposing a test.

Then take this question into the next meeting: "Where does the data the AI needs live, and what may leave your environment?"

Making the test fair

A test like this needs three things agreed in advance: the measure (automated resolution rate), the period and the person who signs the result off. Without them a good result is easy to dismiss and a poor one is easy to explain away. Write the test in the words the team already uses (resolution rate, evaluation set and human in the loop), so everyone reads the result the same way.

Evidence to add

The position remains untested without a source that bears on it. A useful evidence record names the observation, its source, the period and scope, and the person who can confirm it. Include contrary cases and permission for any identifiable account. Until then, use the argument to frame a question rather than claim an outcome.

The bottom line

The short version: record the starting point, agree an approved test on one piece of work, and compare with its limits visible. When someone says "We can build this on a model API ourselves", agree on the test first and review alternative explanations beside the result.

How to read the answer

The tool turns your Topic, Your take, and Target reader into a multi-part article series (The argument, The objection, Who has to agree, What to do next, Evidence to add, and The takeaway), paired with Promotional Posts, a Publishing Order table, and Sector Notes. It does not write full prose paragraphs for you or invent proof; if you leave Proof points or Author background empty, each article marks Evidence to add and Notes for you explains what to supply. Treat the output as a structured editorial spine that needs your real customer examples and author experience before publishing.

What to do next

  • Founder: Add your own lived experience to Author background and fill the Evidence to add block in each article with a real customer baseline and result.
  • CEO: Review The argument and The objection in Article 1 to confirm the point of view challenges the status quo in your market without naming confidential clients.
  • CMO: Schedule the series using the Publishing Order cadence (one article every two weeks) and assign an editor to turn each outline into a finished draft.
  • CRO: Have account executives send Promotional Post 1 and the article link to prospects in the Target reader role who are stuck in "do nothing" or manual processes.
  • Demand generation head: Distribute each published article across LinkedIn, your newsletter, and targeted outbound sequences using the Promotional Posts hooks.
